UK inflation, 2000–2025

Two per cent, then everything else

For two decades, UK inflation mostly stayed close to the Bank of England's target. Then, within a single year, it didn't.

By Your Name · November 2026 · 4 min read

Inflation is the kind of number that matters most when nobody is talking about it. When prices rise steadily and slowly, households and businesses plan around it without a second thought. When they don't, it becomes the only number anyone talks about.

This story follows the UK's annual consumer price inflation rate from 2000 to 2025. It looks at what "normal" looked like, the shocks that broke it, and what a quarter-century of price rises adds up to. Scroll to follow the chart.

2% Since December 2003, the Bank of England's job has been to keep CPI inflation at 2% a year. That's the dashed line, the benchmark for everything that follows.
For most of the 2000s the job looked easy. From 2000 to 2007 inflation averaged just % a year, and it never once went above %.
Then came the first jolts. Oil prices spiked as the financial crisis hit, and inflation reached 3.6% in 2008. In 2011 a VAT rise from 17.5% to 20% and higher commodity prices pushed it to 4.5%, more than double the target.
Then it swung the other way. Oil prices roughly halved between 2014 and 2016, and in 2015 inflation averaged 0.0%. Prices, on average, barely moved at all.
9.1% Then 2022. Energy prices soared, and annual inflation hit 9.1%, more than four times the target. In October 2022 the monthly rate peaked at 11.1%, its highest in over 40 years.
Seen year by year, missing the target is more common than it looks. In of the years inflation ran above 2%, and in of them it was above 3%.

What it means

The headline lesson isn't that the Bank of England "failed". Most of the big misses came from shocks outside its control: oil, tax changes and a global energy crisis. The more useful lesson is about planning. A business or household budget built on "inflation is about 2%" would have been wrong in most years, and badly wrong in 2022 and 2023.

By 2025 inflation had come down a long way from its peak but was still running above target at 3.4%. That's a reminder that the path back to 2% is rarely a straight line.

Methods & sources

Annual figures are the ONS Consumer Prices Index (CPI) 12-month rate, as calendar-year averages. The 2022 monthly peak is the ONS figure for October 2022. The "£100 basket" figure compounds each year's average rate from a 2000 base, so it's an approximation and won't exactly match an index-to-index calculation.
